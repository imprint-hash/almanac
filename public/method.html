Method, and what did not work

Bitget lists 335 tokenised names. Their home markets are open 32.5 hours of every 168, so for about four fifths of the week these things trade with nothing real setting the price. Almanac answers one question about those hours: when a move happens in the dark, how often does it survive the bell?

It never predicts direction, and it never gives advice. It states how often moves like the one in front of you were undone, and it publishes how often those statements have turned out to be true.

How a night is measured

One night is one measurement. The reference is the 16:00 ET close of the session the night belongs to; the move is read at 09:15 ET, fifteen minutes before the bell — not at 09:29, because a fifteen-minute candle closing at 09:30 already contains the first real trades and would smuggle the answer into the question. The outcome is read an hour after the open, at 10:30 ET.

The unit is kept: 1.0 means the move was still exactly there an hour after the bell, 0 means it was entirely undone, above 1.0 means the real market carried it further. A move stood if half of it survived. Both moments shift with daylight saving, so they are computed through the New York zone rather than a fixed offset.

The thing that predicts survival

It is not how big the move is. It is how big the move is for that name — the move divided by what the token covers in an ordinary session, taken as the median high-to-low range of its last ten completed sessions, all of them strictly before the night in question.

Three percent is nothing on a token that ranges eight percent a day. On one that ranges one percent, three percent is news. That ratio was chosen on the first 60% of the record and the band edges fixed there before the rest was looked at.

How it grades itself

Every night in the record is replayed in order. At each one the desk sees only the nights that had already happened, states the chance the move gets undone, and then the morning settles it. The table is rebuilt as history accrues — every 25 nights rather than every night, which can only make the desk look worse, since it is deciding on a table slightly out of date.

It is graded on whether its odds are true, not on how often it was "right". A desk that says one in twenty should be wrong about one time in twenty.

Brier score is the mean squared error of those probabilities against what happened; lower is better. The benchmark is a desk that quotes the overall base rate and knows nothing else. Skill above zero means the bands carry information the base rate does not. Had it come out at or below zero, this page would say so and the bands would be gone.

What did not work

Two approaches were built and measured before this one. Both are here because a method is only worth reading next to the things it beat.

1. Per-name history. It lost to doing nothing.

The first version answered from each name's own record: Micron gives its overnight moves back, Amazon extends them, so quote the name's own survival rate. Measured backwards it looked excellent — median kept ran from 35% on one name to 154% on another.

Graded walk-forward, it scored 74.0% where always saying "it stands" scored 81.5% on the same nights. It was 7.6 points worse than knowing nothing. Its "unwinds" call was right 31% of the time — worse than a coin flip, so it was actively harmful.

The per-name pattern was real in the past and carried no information about the next night. Only the walk-forward test exposed it; the backward-looking table never would have.

2. Five other predictors. Four were noise.

Overnight volume against normal, realised volatility through the night, how early in the night the move arrived, how many dead candles it traded through, and the name's turnover — each was split into quintiles on the training half and tested on data it had never seen.

None beat the benchmark. Only the move-against-normal-day ratio was monotonic on both halves, and only it separated within every raw size bucket — which is what ruled out it being "small moves get erased" wearing a disguise.

Two findings that survived, and surprised us

  • Weekend gaps are calmer, not wilder. 21.3% undone against 25.8% on weeknights, over 333 weekend nights. More unwatched hours did not mean more nonsense.
  • Some of these names have no home market at all. OPENAI, ANTHROPIC, SHEIN and others are private companies. Nothing ever arrives at a bell to correct them, and their moves are undone just 11.6% of the time against 27.3% for names with a real listing behind them.

Limits, stated plainly

  • Ninety days. 2 July to 18 September 2026. One quarter, one market regime. A different regime may move these numbers.
  • Thirty-nine names, the most traded of the 335, so the record leans toward liquid ones. The rail covers 40 names; the bands are what the record measured.
  • Not all of them are stocks. The universe caught commodities, index and leveraged products alongside single names. They are measured the same way, and the ratio treats them on their own terms, but it is worth knowing.
  • One hour. Survival is read at 10:30 ET. A move undone at 14:00 counts as having stood.
  • Direction is not modelled. Almanac has no view on which way a price goes, and will not be given one.
  • No account, no orders, no advice. Public market endpoints only. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell anything.

Where the numbers come from

Bitget's public market API: /api/v2/mix/market/contracts for the universe (a name is tokenised when Bitget's own isRwa flag says so, read live rather than kept as a list here), /history-candles at 15 minutes for every night, and /tickers to order the rail. No key, no account, no private data.

Qwen writes the answer in the question box and nothing else. It is handed a block of figures the desk computed, told it may not do arithmetic or predict direction, and every number it returns is checked against the ones it was given — if a figure appears that nobody handed it, its answer is discarded and the desk's own wording is served, with a line on the page saying that happened.
